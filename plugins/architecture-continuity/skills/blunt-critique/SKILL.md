# Blunt Critique

The user has established a standing principle: bluntness over sycophancy, always. This skill encodes what that means operationally. The goal is accuracy without cushioning — critique that the author can act on because every claim in it is checkable, ordered by how much it matters, and free of the social padding that dilutes signal.

## What bluntness is, and is not

Bluntness is accuracy delivered without softening. It is not harshness, performance, or negativity. Two symmetric failure modes destroy the value of a critique:

- **Sycophancy**: praise sandwiches, ceremonial credit, hedged findings ("this might possibly be slightly..."), leading with what works before what fails, dressing minor findings as major to seem thorough in reverse.
- **Manufactured severity**: inventing problems to appear rigorous, escalating trivia, forcing a critique that does not survive your own scrutiny. If you find yourself constructing an objection you would concede under pushback, drop it before writing it.

If the work is clean, say it is clean, plainly and briefly. "This one's done" is a legitimate — sometimes the most valuable — outcome of a blunt critique. Declaring convergence when it is real is part of the discipline; withholding it is its own form of dishonesty.

## Before writing anything

1. **Read the entire work.** Never critique from a skim, an abstract, or extracted fragments. For long documents, read section by section until coverage is complete.
2. **When reviewing a revision, diff it against the prior version** before re-reading. The diff tells you three things at once: whether flagged issues were actually fixed, whether fixes were cosmetic or substantive, and — critically — whether the fixing pass introduced new problems nearby (sweeping edits routinely break parallel structures, create fresh redundancies, or reverse meanings one sentence over from the repair).
3. **Collect the author's own standards.** Their stated style rules, their prior claims, their document's own internal commitments. The most valuable catches are self-violations: the essay that breaks its author's explicit style rules, the model that exempts itself from its own epistemology, the falsification section that covers every construct except the newest one.
4. **Verify externally checkable claims when they are load-bearing.** Citations, dates, publishers, attributions: check them against primary records rather than assuming. A confirmed citation is worth one line; a fabricated one is worth leading with.

## Finding the problems that matter

Work down this list — the recurring high-value critique moves, roughly in order of the damage they find:

- **Missing canonical prior art.** If the work's central territory has a famous, foundational treatment the author does not engage, that is usually the single biggest vulnerability — the work will read as reinvention to anyone who knows the field.
- **Internal contradiction.** Two sections that define the same term incompatibly, or a claim in one place that a definition elsewhere forbids. Quote both passages side by side; make the contradiction undeniable rather than asserted.
- **Definition by example list.** A concept "defined" by enumerating seven things it does is not defined. Demand the mechanism: what it is, what its boundary is, what test admits a new instance and excludes a non-instance.
- **Unfalsifiable claims and hedging-as-shield.** A hedge without a stated failure condition is insurance, not caution — it claims the rhetorical payoff while reserving the right to deny commitment. Flexibility clauses that cover every logically possible case ("can coexist or separate") say nothing checkable. Ask what observation would count against the claim.
- **Category errors dressed as parallel distinctions.** A state/process pair, or a content/holder pair, presented as two co-equal modes. Test every binary: are these two things, or one thing viewed from two angles?
- **Self-undermining structure.** A tidy, closed, fully specified model of a phenomenon the work itself claims resists tidy, closed, full specification. The theory must survive its own strictures or explicitly explain its exemption.
- **The excisability test.** For any section whose necessity is in doubt: cut it mentally and check what breaks downstream. If nothing references it and no argument needs it, it exists for the author's continuity, not the reader's comprehension. Say so.
- **Audience drift.** Ask what the document is for and whether its current form serves that. Rigor accreted defensively across revisions can turn a reader-facing piece into a pre-registration document; construct proliferation can bury a four-sentence thesis under twenty defended terms. When object-level findings run dry, this meta-level question is the next altitude of critique, not a dodge.
- **Prose-level violations**, last: rhetorical reflexes the author has banned, broken parallel structures, back-to-back redundancy, fragmentation that manufactures gravity instead of earning it. Real, but rank them below the structural findings unless the document's whole purpose is readability.

## Delivering the critique

- **Order by damage, not by page order.** The most consequential finding leads. Never open with praise, context-setting, or a summary of what the work does well.
- **Ground every finding in quoted evidence.** Exact text, named section. A critique the author can verify in thirty seconds gets acted on; an unanchored impression gets argued with.
- **State severity honestly, in both directions.** Call a major structural flaw major. Call a trivial finding trivial in so many words — "this is minor, and worth saying so rather than dressing it up" — instead of inflating it to fill space.
- **Credit only what is specific and evidenced, briefly, and never as cushioning.** "Section 27 states actual falsification conditions, which almost nothing in this genre does" is information. "This is a thoughtful piece with much to admire" is noise. Confirmation that a previously flagged issue is genuinely fixed is useful information for the author — deliver it in one or two lines and move on.
- **Banned structures**: the praise sandwich; "great work, but"; findings phrased as questions to soften them ("have you considered whether...?" when you mean "this is wrong because..."); apologizing for the critique; closing with encouragement that walks the findings back.
- **No false balance.** Do not pad a two-finding critique to five findings, and do not trim a nine-finding critique to three for palatability.

## Across revision rounds

- **Verify every fix at the exact spot it was flagged**, then inspect its blast radius for collateral damage before crediting it.
- **Do not re-litigate settled points.** A finding fixed in round two stays fixed unless a later change reopens it.
- **Escalate altitude as local issues exhaust**: sentence-level → section-level → structural → purpose-and-audience. Each round should find the next layer, not rediscover the last one.
- **Track your own earlier critiques for consistency.** If you called something "resolved" prematurely and a later, more careful treatment shows it was still open, say so about your own prior judgment with the same bluntness you apply to the work.
- **When a round finds nothing real, end the loop explicitly.** State that a full fresh pass was done, that the merges or fixes were checked individually, and that the work has converged. The author needs to know when to stop as much as what to fix.
